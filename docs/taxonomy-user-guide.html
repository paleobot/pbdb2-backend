A field guide to pbdb2

The Taxonomy Ledger

How the next Paleobiology Database keeps track of fossil names — and decides, on its own, what everyone currently believes.

In the old system, a curator typed in a paper's opinion about a name, and once a day a slow background job re-read every opinion ever entered and rebuilt its best guess at "the" classification. The new system does the same job — but the bookkeeping is simpler, the answer is always current, and nothing is ever silently overwritten.

The big idea

Notes go in. A ledger comes out. Nobody edits the ledger.

Everything in this system rests on keeping two things strictly separate: what a published paper said, and what the database currently believes.

Think of every published taxonomic claim — "this species belongs in that genus," "these two names are the same animal," "this name was misspelled" — as a small note clipped to an index card. Nobody ever deletes a note, even a note from a hundred years ago that everyone now disagrees with. It's part of the historical record: someone, somewhere, published that claim.

A separate, invisible ledger holds the current answer for every name: is it accepted, what does it belong to, is it a synonym of something else. It's entirely recomputed from the notes — the same way a librarian re-derives "the current consensus" by reading through a card's whole file rather than trusting something scrawled on the outside of the folder.

That's the whole trick. If the ledger and the notes ever disagree, the notes win, and the ledger gets rebuilt. The idea of what is "correct" is not static.

The unit of identity

Every exact spelling gets its own card, forever

The single most important rule in the new system: a card's name and rank never change. If either changes, that's a different card.

Once a card is written — say, Roveacrinus alata, species — nobody ever edits its name field. If a later paper decides the ending should be -us instead of -a to match Latin grammar, that correction doesn't touch the old card. It creates a brand-new card, Roveacrinus alatus, and a note links the two together: "this is a corrected form of that."

Follow enough of those linking notes — corrections, regenderings, rank changes, genus reassignments — and you get a whole chain of cards that are all, at bottom, the same published name going through its history of respellings. The system calls that a lineage, and it picks one card from the chain to be the one currently in use.

If you know Classic Classic's taxon_no did two jobs at once: it named one exact spelling, and it was supposed to trace back to "the" original combination — a distinction that got blurry in practice. The new system splits those apart cleanly: a card (permid) is always one exact spelling. Which spelling is "the original one" for a lineage is worked out separately, automatically, from the correction notes — never stored as a fact on the card itself.
What gets clipped to a card

Three kinds of notes, three different jobs

Every published taxonomic claim is one of exactly three kinds. Keeping them apart is what makes the recompute step tractable.

Spelling & sameness

Is this card a corrected, respelled, or renamed form of another card? Or is it a wholly different name for the same animal — a synonym?

e.g. "alata is a correction of alatus" · "scalper is a synonym of sulcatus"

⊂

Placement

What higher group does this name belong to? A species in a genus, a genus in a family — one card containing another.

e.g. "Dorudon belongs to Basilosauridae"

?

Status

A note purely about the name's own standing — is it too poorly known to use, or was it published without following the rules?

e.g. "nomen dubium" · "nomen nudum"

Placement notes are read a little differently from the other two — but only above the species level. For a genus or higher, a synonym doesn't lose its own placement claims just because it's no longer the "main" name — the system pools placement notes across every name judged to be the same taxon, and lets the best-evidenced one win, even if it happens to be filed under a junior synonym. That mirrors how a working taxonomist actually reasons about where something belongs. At the species level, though, only the accepted name's own placement notes are read; a junior synonym's containment claims stay filed under that synonym and are never borrowed.

Breaking ties

When notes disagree, three questions settle it

Two papers can make opposite claims about the same name. The recompute step needs one deterministic way to pick a winner — every time, for every kind of note.

FIRST

Was evidence given?

A claim backed by an argument — a described character, an analysis — outranks a claim that's just asserted.

THEN

Which is newer?

Among equally well-argued claims, the most recently published one reflects the current state of the science.

FINALLY

Which was entered later?

A last-resort tiebreaker for the rare case where evidence and year both tie exactly.

That's it — the same three questions, asked in the same order, for spelling, for placement, and for status, independently. Merging two names into one concept (Case 3, next) runs the identical three questions too, just with one extra check ahead of them: whichever name was never itself accused of being the junior one gets first refusal, before evidence even gets asked. There's no other special-case logic hiding anywhere, and no daily batch job: the moment a new note is entered, this ranking runs and the ledger is brought current. There's no overnight wait to see whether a name's classification changed.

Worked examples

Case files

Five examples, each adding one more piece of the mechanism — all drawn from real published names, checked directly against the live system rather than invented for the page.

Case 1spelling · correction

Roveacrinus alata → alatus

A grammatical correction, matching the species epithet's ending to standard Latin usage.

A card exists for Roveacrinus alata. A later paper corrects the ending to -us. That correction is entered as its own card, linked back to the original with a "correction of" note — not an edit to the first card.

Both cards are eligible to be the lineage's current spelling. Since the corrected form is the newer, better-argued claim, it wins the ranking and becomes the name actually shown as current — while the original spelling stays on record, permanently, as part of the same lineage.

The point: a correction is a competition between two eligible cards. The newer one usually wins, but it has to earn that — nothing about "being a correction" makes it automatic.
Case 2spelling · misspelling

Sinraptor dongii vs. Sinraptor dongi

A 2007 paper flagged the double-i spelling of this well-known Jurassic theropod as an error, not a new name.

This genus's real history has three cards, not two. It was first named Yangchuanosaurus dongi in 1993; a 1999 paper reassigned it to a different genus, linking it forward as a legitimate recombination — Sinraptor dongi, the name still in use. Separately, a 2007 paper noted that Sinraptor dongii — the same name with an extra i, the usual Latin-declension slip — had been showing up in the literature, and flagged it as a plain misspelling, not a new name or a rival spelling.

That note links the two cards, exactly like Case 1's correction note does. But it's flagged differently: a misspelling is marked so that it can never win the "current spelling" ranking, no matter how much evidence or how many years pass in its favor. The recombination is a fair competitor; the misspelling is disqualified from the race entirely — and, checked directly against the live ledger, that's exactly the current state: Sinraptor dongi is the accepted spelling, with both the older genus assignment and the double-i error linked into the same lineage underneath it.

Checked against Classic Classic's own opinions table reaches the identical conclusion independently: taxon_no 138973 ("Sinraptor dongii") carries spelling_reason = 'misspelling', pointing at the same Sinraptor dongi that pbdb2's own ledger currently names as accepted.
The point: not every linking note makes its card eligible to become the accepted name. Misspellings are linked into the lineage — so searches and downloads still find them — but they can't win it.
Case 3sameness · synonymy

Taeniolabis scalper and sulcatus, folded into Taeniolabis taoensis

Cope named the same specimen twice — sulcatus in 1882, then scalper in 1884 — the start of a synonymy tangle curators kept adding to for another century.

Neither of Cope's two names is the same spelling of one name — they're two entirely different, independently published names that turned out to describe the same animal. A "same taxon as" note merges their two lineages into one group the system calls a concept. That much hasn't changed since this system was designed.

What has changed is the concept itself. It didn't stop growing in 1884. Between then and 1987, curators entered synonymy opinions folding in four more originally-separate names — Polymastodon attenuatus, P. latimolis, P. selenodus, and Catopsalis pollux — plus a third Cope name, Taeniolabis taoensis, that both scalper and sulcatus eventually got filed as junior to as well. Today the ledger's current answer for this whole nine-name tangle is Taeniolabis taoensis — not sulcatus. Both of Cope's original names are now recorded as junior synonyms of it.

The rule that decides a winner hasn't changed — the same evidence-then-recency contest, with one check ahead of it: whichever name was never itself accused of being the junior side of a currently-standing synonymy note gets first refusal. What changed is simply how much evidence that contest now has to weigh: a two-name illustration from 1882–1884 is, in the live data, one small corner of a century-long argument. The system doesn't freeze an example in place — the next opinion entered can always reshape a concept this size again.

The point: a lineage groups spellings of one published name. A concept groups multiple, separately published names that turned out to be the same taxon — and unlike a lineage, a concept can keep absorbing more names indefinitely as more opinions are entered. A concept's membership and its accepted name are both live answers, not fixed facts.
Case 4status · recency

Stagodon tumidus — a 1929 doubt, outlasted

Marsh named it in 1889. A 1929 monograph called it a nomen dubium; a 1966 paper's own status note about it didn't repeat that doubt.

Two papers, decades apart, each filed an ordinary status note about this one name — neither one framed as a response to the other. In 1929, American Mesozoic Mammalia called it a nomen dubium: too poorly known to use with confidence, no evidence stated. In 1966, a paper on the Lance Formation's marsupials filed its own status note — nomen oblitum, a name that's simply fallen out of use — also with no evidence stated.

Neither note mentions the other. Both are just ordinary testimony about this name's standing, competing the same way every other note on this page competes: evidence tied, so recency decides. The 1966 note is newer, and nomen oblitum doesn't invalidate a name the way nomen dubium does — so, checked directly against the live ledger, Stagodon tumidus stands today with no disqualifying status attached. The 1929 doubt is still on record. It's simply no longer the top-ranked word on what this name's standing is.

The point: because the ledger is never anything but a recomputation, there's no separate "undo" mechanism to write or to get wrong. A later opinion doesn't have to reject an earlier one to outrank it — ordinary competing testimony, entered independently, is all reversal ever takes. If a future paper revives the 1929 doubt with real evidence behind it, the ledger updates the moment that's entered, the same as every other case here.
Case 5status · nomen dubium

Testudo pertenuis — doubted out of the ledger

Cope named it in 1892; a 2018 review of North American fossil turtles (Pan-Testudinoidea) called it a nomen dubium, with no evidence cited.

Testudo pertenuis has a real paper trail: four separate papers, 1893 through 2018, tie it to a companion spelling, Testudo pertenius, and a 1982 paper — this one with stated evidence — placed it as a junior synonym of Gopherus canyonensis. None of that made the name disappear on its own. But the same 2018 review that last touched its spelling also filed a status note on it: nomen dubium, no evidence given.

Checked directly against the ledger today, neither spelling of this name has a current, accepted row. The lineage is currently absent — not deleted, not hidden, just not eligible to be anyone's answer to "what is this name currently." Every opinion listed above is still on record exactly as published; nothing about this outcome required deleting or overriding any of it.

The point: the rule described above isn't theoretical — it has already decided a real name's current status. And because the ledger is only ever a recomputation, this isn't final: the next time someone enters a paper defending pertenuis with real evidence, the name is back the moment that opinion is entered, the same as every other case on this page.
For readers who know the old system

What's actually different from Classic

The underlying philosophy — publish an opinion, let the database derive belief — carries over unchanged. What changed is how faithfully the machinery lives up to that philosophy.

Classic
2.0

A name's identity conflated "this exact spelling" with "the original combination it traces back to" — two different questions, one column.

A card is always exactly one spelling, one rank, forever. Which spelling is "original" is worked out separately, on demand.

The classification tree was rebuilt by a background daemon polling for changes every couple of seconds, with a full rebuild "weekly, to be safe." New opinions could take until the next day to show up correctly.

The ledger recomputes as part of entering the note. There's nothing to wait for overnight.

Reading a taxon's current classification could itself trigger a write to the cache — a "read" that silently mutated the database.

Reads only ever look at the ledger as it currently stands. Nothing about looking something up changes what's stored.

Undoing a synonymy or a misspelling call meant walking back through cached history to find the last clean state — logic that had to handle its own edge cases (loops, partial updates) separately from everything else.

There is no walk-back logic. A reversal is a new note; the next recompute produces the reverted state as a side effect of being correct.

Evidence was graded on a four-step scale — stated with evidence, stated without, implied, second-hand — read off a hand-written SQL CASE expression repeated in several places.

Evidence is simply yes or no: was a supporting argument given. The three middle Classic grades collapse into "no" — a deliberate simplification, with publication year doing correspondingly more of the tie-breaking work.

A rootless "belongs to" opinion — no containing group given — was effectively dropped; there was nowhere clean for it to live.

A rootless placement note is kept as a real, ranked claim: "this reference asserted no containing group," which can still win or lose against a later, better opinion.

nomen dubium, nomen nudum, and similar status calls all competed in the same reliability-ranked pool as ordinary placement and synonymy opinions — the system couldn't tell doubt about a name apart from an act that actually invalidates it.

Nomen dubium, nomen vanum, and nomen nudum are all treated the same way, and all three can disqualify a card — but only as a reversible veto, never an automatic one (see Case 5, a real name this has already happened to). A card is ranked into the ordinary contest first, ignoring status entirely; only if the status note's own evidence and year actually outrank the card's best competing claim does the card get evicted, with the ranking rerun among whoever's left. (The Code doesn't distinguish nomen vanum from nomen dubium at all, and old curatorial tagging of nomen nudum can't be trusted to have always applied its stricter test correctly — so none of the three gets to be an absolute, unappealable bar the way a misspelling is.) If every card in a lineage ends up disqualified this way — or all are misspellings — with nothing left standing, that lineage produces no "current name" at all, rather than crowning an unavailable one by default. The same rule cascades one level up: a synonym group with no eligible member anywhere simply doesn't appear in the ledger either. Nothing is deleted — the notes are still all there — there's just nothing available to call current belief.
